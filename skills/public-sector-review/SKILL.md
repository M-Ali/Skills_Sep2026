# Public sector category review

Research a government or state-owned client from primary sources, and write the
review in a register that survives being read by the people who made the
decisions you are describing.

Two things make this different from commercial research, and both are load-
bearing:

- **The organisation may not need to persuade anyone to survive.** Statute,
  mandate or ownership often guarantees its revenue. That changes what its
  behaviour means, and misreading it produces confident nonsense.
- **The room includes the decision-makers.** A public sector panel may contain
  board members, ministry officials and the people personally responsible for
  what you are about to characterise. Language that reads as incisive in an
  agency review reads as an accusation across that table.

## The order matters more than the effort

1. **The annual report and the statute.** First, before any observation about
   what the organisation lacks.
2. **The institutions around it** — line ministry, regulator, competition
   authority, auditor, trade association.
3. **Comparable state bodies**, at home and abroad.
4. **The review document.**

Most bad public sector reviews start from the website, conclude the
organisation is neglectful, and are wrong in a way the client notices in the
first minute.

---

## Phase 1 — the annual report and the statute

**Read the annual report before anything else.** Public sector bodies are
usually required to publish one, and it is board-approved and current in a way
the website is not.

Extract, verbatim where it is stated as such:

- **the vision, mission, strategy and objectives** — these exist far more often
  than the website suggests, and quoting them proves you did the work
- the audited numbers, and any multi-year summary table
- ratings, and who issued them and when
- segment performance
- named counterparties, panels, partners
- the chairman's and chief executive's statements — they name the priorities
- anything disclosed voluntarily

**Then find the enabling instrument.** The statute, ordinance, act or SRO that
creates the body and defines its mandate. Search for the organisation's name
with "act", "ordinance", "SRO", "statutory", "rules". You are looking for:

- what it is *required* to do
- what it is *protected* in doing — a guaranteed share, a first right of
  refusal, an exclusive licence, a compulsory cession
- who it reports to

**Why this comes first.** Judging a public body by its website and then telling
it what it lacks is how you assert that an organisation has no stated vision
while it is printed on page two of the report it publishes every year. That
mistake ends a meeting. It has happened.

Where the website understates what the report shows — a rating years out of
date, an achievements page frozen a decade ago — **that is a finding about the
website, not about the organisation.** Report it that way round.

## Phase 2 — the institutions

Public sector clients sit inside a dense institutional environment, and most of
it publishes.

- **The line ministry.** Policy statements, budget documents, annual plans.
- **The sector regulator.** Statistics, rule changes, live consultations.
- **The competition authority.** Search this explicitly — see below.
- **The auditor general / public accounts committee.** Findings on state bodies
  are public in many jurisdictions and are unusually candid.
- **The trade association.** Even a state monopoly usually sits within an
  industry that has one, and it often publishes the official market sizing.
- **Procurement portals.** Tender notices reveal current priorities, and are
  sometimes the only genuinely current stream a body publishes.

**The competition authority is the highest-value source in this method and it
almost never appears in a general search.** Look for market studies, state-of-
competition reports and sector inquiries. They are independent, they size market
shares — often using data the organisation itself supplied — and they say things
about a state body that it would never publish about itself.

Expect them to contain three things worth having:

| What | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| An independent market share | Frequently contradicts the client's own published figure |
| Recommendations to remove protection | Live commercial risk the client is living with |
| The client's own best argument, made for them | Often the authority states the public-interest case and then sets it aside |

## Phase 3 — comparable bodies

Compare against organisations shaped like the client, not against commercial
giants.

- **Structural peers abroad** — the same function in another country: same
  ownership model, same mandate, same protection. These carry the
  recommendation, because their behaviour is achievable.
- **Other state bodies at home** — they share the same governance and approval
  constraints, so what they manage to publish is a realistic benchmark.
- **Commercial leaders in the same sector** — useful for establishing the
  category norm, not as a target.

For each, read the annual report, the positioning in their own words, and what
recurring published assets they own.

## The mechanism — why protected bodies go quiet

This is usually the central insight, and getting it right is the difference
between a useful review and an insulting one.

When an organisation's revenue is secured by statute, the commercial need to
persuade is correspondingly lower. Silence is then **rational**, not negligent.
An organisation competing without that protection publishes results, ratings and
analysis because it must.

State it that way. It is more accurate, and it lets you make the recommendation
without implying anyone has been failing at their job.

**Then find what is not protected.** This is where the argument lives:

- the share of business *beyond* the mandated minimum
- terms negotiated with counterparties who are not compelled to deal
- the confidence of the ministry and the legislature
- recruitment against private sector competitors
- policy standing when protection is under review

Quantify it if you can. A statutory floor of 35% means 65% is contested — and if
the mandated portion turns out to be a minority of actual revenue, say so with
the source. A protected body that discovers most of its book is competitive has
a reason to act.

---

## Register — this is not optional here

- **Observe, do not characterise.** "The most recent item published is dated
  2012" — not "fourteen years stale". The date does the work.
- **Place a shortcoming against the category first.** If comparable state bodies
  behave the same way, say so before mentioning the client. The finding is then
  structural, and nobody in the room is being blamed.
- **Never diminish a peer.** Counterpart national bodies may be known personally
  to the panel.
- **Explain by mechanism, never by neglect.**
- **Handle live policy matters once, neutrally, and move on.** If the competition
  authority has recommended removing the client's protection, the panel knows far
  more about it than you do. Reference it as a reason the work is timely. Do not
  present it as a discovery, do not characterise the authority, do not speculate
  on the outcome. Keep the detail in the internal review, not the client-facing
  deck.
- **Do not propose replacing an approved corporate idea.** If the board has
  adopted a vision and mission, build on them. Proposing a replacement to the
  people who approved them is a way to lose the room.

A finding framed as an opportunity gets acted on. The same finding framed as a
failure gets argued with.

## Measures — what success looks like here

Public sector bodies frequently have no retail customer, so consumer metrics are
not merely weak, they are meaningless. Awareness scores tell a state reinsurer
nothing it can act on.

Propose measures the organisation can use:

- standing among the counterparties who choose to deal with it
- citation of its published analysis by the regulator, industry and press
- policy confidence — is it consulted, is it referenced
- recruitment quality against private sector competitors
- currency of its own published record

If a mass campaign is in scope, report its reach figures because it was bought on
them — but do not offer reach as the measure of whether the strategy worked.

## Evidence rules

**Two grades, never blended.** A figure you read yourself is *retrieved*; a
figure from a search summary or secondary article is *reported*. Mark which, and
never promote one without reading the source. Snippets are routinely stale.

**Every claim carries its source and retrieval date**, on the line.

**Verify the client's own published claims.** Expect three outcomes:

| Outcome | What to do |
|---|---|
| Confirmed, basis known | Use it, and state the basis every time |
| Cannot be reconciled | Do not use it; ask the client for the basis |
| Contradicted by a credible third party | Do not use it; flag it plainly |

The third case is common with state bodies, whose website figures are often old
and unsourced. A client claiming 45% where the competition authority says 25-27%
— using the client's own data — is a real example. Repeating the client's figure
back to them would have been repeating an error into the one room able to
correct it.

**State the denominator.** The same numerator against a different base gives a
different percentage. If both are defensible, give both.

**What you could not verify is a finding.** A short, specific list of open
questions is a credential.

## Document structure

```markdown
# [Client] — public sector category review
Compiled [date]. Every claim is sourced; register at the end.

## How to read this
Evidence vs inference. What could not be verified.

## 1. Mandate and standing
What the statute requires and protects. The corporate idea, verbatim.
Audited performance. Ratings. Segments. Counterparties.

## 2. What the organisation publishes
Each owned channel, what it last carried, when. Observed, not characterised.
Where the website understates the annual report, say so.

## 3. The institutional environment
Ministry, regulator, competition authority, auditor, association.
What each publishes. Any live policy matter affecting the client.

## 4. The category
Size, structure, the client's place in it, with the denominator stated.

## 5. Comparable bodies
Structural peers abroad; other state bodies at home; commercial leaders as norm.

## 6. Protected and contested
What the mandate guarantees. What has to be earned. Quantified.

## 7. The mechanism
Why the category behaves as it does, sourced. What it changes.

## 8. Verified, contradicted, open
The client's published claims, each marked. Then the open questions.

## Source register
```

## Before you deliver

- Annual report and enabling statute read, not skimmed
- Competition authority searched explicitly
- Trade association found, or its absence confirmed
- Every figure sourced and dated; retrieved and reported separated
- Client's own claims checked, contradictions flagged
- Protected vs contested revenue quantified
- Language checked: nothing characterises, nothing blames
- Live policy matters handled once and neutrally
- Open questions listed
