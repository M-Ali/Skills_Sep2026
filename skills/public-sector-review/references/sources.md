# Finding the sources — public sector

Where the primary material for a government or state-owned client lives. The
good sources here are rarely the ones that rank first, and several of them never
surface unless you search for them by name.

## The annual report

Look on the organisation's own site under: Investor Relations, Financial
Reports, Annual Report, Reports and Accounts, Downloads, Publications, Media.

```
"<organisation>" annual report <year> filetype:pdf
"<organisation>" financial reports by year
site:<domain> annual report
```

If listed, the stock exchange carries filings — often more reliably than the
organisation's own site.

**Take the most recent full-year report.** Quarterlies lack the multi-year
summary tables, the strategy statements and the governance sections that make the
rest of the review possible.

**A report that is hard to find is not absent.** These pages are frequently
buried under a year index several levels deep. Follow the index.

## The enabling statute

Every statutory body is created by something. Find it.

```
"<organisation>" act ordinance statute
"<organisation>" SRO ministry
"<organisation>" established under
<country> <sector> ordinance <year>
```

You are looking for what the organisation is **required** to do, what it is
**protected** in doing, and **who it answers to**. Protection takes forms worth
recognising: a guaranteed minimum share, an exclusive first right of refusal, a
compulsory cession, a sole licence, a reserved category of business.

The statute is also where you learn that a protection everyone describes loosely
is actually narrower than assumed — applying only to one class of business, for
instance, while the majority of revenue comes from elsewhere. That distinction
is often the most useful thing in the whole review.

## The line ministry

```
ministry of <sector> <country> <organisation>
<ministry> annual plan <year>
<ministry> policy <sector>
```

Ministry policy documents and budget statements say what the government wants
from the organisation, which is not always what the organisation says about
itself.

## The sector regulator

```
<sector> regulator <country>
<sector> authority <country> statistics
<country> <sector> rules <year>
```

Regulators publish sector statistics, rule changes and live consultations. A
consultation that would change the client's position is material to any
recommendation.

## The competition authority

**Search this explicitly and separately.** It is the highest-value source in this
method and it almost never appears in a general search for the organisation.

```
competition commission <country> <sector> report
antitrust <country> <sector> market study
<sector> "state of competition" <country>
competition authority <country> state-owned enterprises
```

Why it is worth the specific effort:

- **Independent** of both the organisation and its association.
- **Sizes market shares**, often using data the organisation itself supplied,
  which makes the figure very hard to dispute.
- Says things about a state body that it would never publish about itself,
  including criticism.
- Reveals **live policy risk** — recommendations to remove statutory protection.
- Frequently **states the organisation's own public-interest argument** and then
  sets it against competition policy. That argument, sitting unmade by the
  client, is often the strongest thing you will find.

A single market study can invert a review: contradicting a published market
share, revealing that most of the business is competitive rather than protected,
and showing the protection is under recommendation for removal — none of which is
visible anywhere else.

**Handle what you find here carefully in client-facing work.** See the register
section of SKILL.md.

## Audit and legislative oversight

```
auditor general report <organisation>
public accounts committee <organisation>
<country> audit report state-owned enterprises
```

Audit findings on state bodies are public in many jurisdictions and unusually
candid. Treat them as you would competition authority material: valuable, and to
be handled once and neutrally.

## The trade association

Even a statutory monopoly usually sits inside an industry that has one.

```
<sector> association <country>
<sector> federation <country>
"<sector>" industry statistics <country>
```

Look for a **year book or annual review** — it often carries the official market
sizing, segment by segment, and penetration against GDP. Associations also
frequently list the state body separately in their market tables, which gives you
an independent figure for its share.

**Find the association before claiming anything is unpublished.** It may already
publish what you were about to recommend the client produce. That does not kill
the recommendation; it sharpens it into "they publish X, only the client can
publish Y" — and makes the association a partner rather than a rival.

## Procurement portals

```
<organisation> tender notices
<country> public procurement authority <organisation>
```

Tender notices reveal current priorities and active projects. For some
organisations this is the only genuinely current public stream — which is itself
a finding about how they communicate.

## Ratings

```
"<organisation>" rating report filetype:pdf
"<organisation>" credit rating <year>
```

Rating reports give an independent analyst's view of the business, its segments
and its competitive position, usually with several years of figures. They also
confirm the **current** rating, which organisation websites frequently report
years out of date.

## What to record

For every source:

- the **URL**, and a local copy of anything that might move
- the **retrieval date**
- **retrieved** (you read it) or **reported** (a summary said so)
- what it is **authoritative for** — an association on market size, a rating
  agency on financial strength, a competition authority on market share, none of
  them on each other

Local copies matter more than they look: publication URLs change, and a figure
you cannot reopen is a figure you cannot defend when it is questioned weeks
later.
