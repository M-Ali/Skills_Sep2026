# Consumer brand category review

Research a consumer-facing brand and its category from primary sources, and
write down what is actually true with the source attached.

Consumer categories differ from institutional ones in a way that shapes the
whole method: **the client competes for a decision that a member of the public
makes, repeatedly, against alternatives.** So the review has to answer not just
what the company is, but what the category says to people, who it says it to,
and what nobody is saying.

## The order matters more than the effort

1. **The annual report and investor material.** First, before any observation
   about what the brand lacks.
2. **The category's measurement** — regulator statistics, industry association,
   retail or subscriber data. Size it before you characterise it.
3. **The competitors** — their filings, their positioning, and what they are
   actually publishing to consumers.
4. **The review document.**

Starting from advertising and working backwards produces a review that is all
impression and no structure. Size the category first; the creative reads
differently once you know who is growing.

---

## Phase 1 — the annual report and investor material

**Read the annual report before anything else**, and for a listed brand the
investor deck and analyst briefing too. They are current, board-approved, and
usually franker about competitive pressure than any marketing material.

Extract:

- the stated vision, mission, strategy and objectives, **verbatim**
- revenue, profit and margin, with a multi-year series if there is a summary
- **segment and brand-level performance** — which brands or products grew and
  which shrank, which is often invisible from outside
- volume vs value growth, if disclosed — price-led growth and volume growth mean
  very different things for a brand
- distribution reach, store or outlet counts, subscriber numbers, active users
- marketing or A&P spend if disclosed, and its trend
- what management names as the competitive threat

Investor material frequently states market share and category growth that the
brand's own marketing never publishes. It is also where you find out that a
brand the market thinks is winning is buying its growth with price.

## Phase 2 — sizing the category

Do this before forming any view about the advertising.

- **The sector regulator.** Central banks publish banking statistics; telecom
  authorities publish subscriber and ARPU data; insurance and securities
  regulators publish market statistics. These are the official numbers.
- **The industry association.** Search `<category> association <country>`. Look
  for a year book or annual review — it often carries the official market sizing,
  segment by segment, and penetration against population or GDP.
- **Retail and panel measurement**, where available — value and volume share,
  penetration, frequency, distribution.
- **Penetration** is the number that reframes a category. A market at low
  penetration is underdeveloped, not contested, and growth need not come from a
  competitor. That changes the strategic options fundamentally.

**Check the client's claimed share against these.** Brands publish shares that
are old, differently based, or wrong. State the denominator every time: the same
numerator against a different base gives a different percentage, and "18% of the
market" without a basis is not a fact.

## Phase 3 — the competitors

Two passes, and both matter.

**The commercial pass.** For each significant competitor: annual report, revenue
and share trend, distribution footprint, pricing posture, recent launches, and
what management says its strategy is.

**The communications pass.** What is each brand actually saying to consumers
right now? Collect their live output — advertising, owned channels, social — and
code it consistently so the comparison means something.

For the communications pass, use the sibling skills rather than reinventing
them:

- **`category-creative-scan`** — collects competitors' live social creative, codes
  every asset on a nine-dimension schema, and produces a brand-by-brand deck plus
  the gap analysis. This is the right tool for "what is the category posting".
- **`competitor-comms-audit`** — the strategic layer: message-territory mapping,
  share of voice against share of market, and how to write the finding
  defensibly.

What to look for across the two passes together:

- **Convergence.** When several brands funnel every message into the same place —
  a rewards app, a price message, a category benefit — that is the category's
  centre of gravity, and it is usually invisible on a claim map because the
  claims differ while the destination is identical.
- **Vacancy.** A message territory that is credible, ownable and unoccupied.
- **Abandonment.** A segment the whole category has drifted away from. Categories
  drift young; the segment with the money often stops being addressed.
- **Share of voice against share of market.** A brand overspending relative to
  its share is buying growth; one underspending is harvesting.

## Look for the mechanism

A pattern is an observation; the mechanism behind it is the insight.

When every brand in a category behaves the same way, ask what structural fact
makes it rational. In consumer categories the answer is usually in the plumbing:

- **Distribution economics** — who owns the shelf, the branch, the airtime seller
- **Channel dependence** — bancassurance, modern trade, telco distribution
- **Customer ownership** — a brand that does not own its customer relationship
  behaves very differently from one that does
- **Regulation** — pricing rules, disclosure requirements, advertising codes
- **Category maturity** — penetration determines whether growth comes from new
  users or from a competitor

Two tests before it goes in the document: **can you cite it**, and **does it
change the recommendation**? A mechanism that leaves the advice unchanged is
trivia. The useful ones tell the client which competitor behaviours to ignore,
because they solve a problem the client does not have.

---

## Evidence rules

**Two grades, never blended.** A figure you read yourself is *retrieved*; one
from a search summary or a secondary article is *reported*. Mark which. Search
snippets are routinely stale — follower counts, store counts and shares taken
from summaries are frequently out of date, and accounts that a snippet still
describes may no longer exist.

**Every claim carries its source and retrieval date.**

**Verify the client's claimed share.** Three outcomes, handled differently:

| Outcome | What to do |
|---|---|
| Confirmed, basis known | Use it, always with the basis stated |
| Cannot be reconciled | Do not use it; ask the client for the basis |
| Contradicted by a credible source | Do not use it; flag it plainly |

**Never present share of voice from an unequal or capped sample.** If you
collected the same number of posts per brand, you have measured your own
collection, not the category. Say so before presenting anything else.

**Separate organic from paid.** Owned-channel output says nothing about media
weight. Do not let one stand in for the other.

**Label inference as inference.** Reading a target audience off casting and
styling is legitimate and useful — and it is a read of who the ad is *aimed at*,
never a fact about who buys.

---

## Register

Consumer categories permit more directness than public sector work — but the
client still made the decisions you are describing, and the incumbent agency may
be in the room.

- Observe before you judge. Give the number and the date, then the read.
- Criticise the work, not the people.
- Credit small brands honestly — the sharpest work in a category often comes from
  the smallest advertiser, and a review that only flatters the leaders is not a
  review.
- Where a client's own behaviour is the finding, put it against the category
  first so it reads as a pattern rather than an accusation.

## Document structure

```markdown
# [Brand] — category review
Compiled [date]. Every claim is sourced; register at the end.

## How to read this
Evidence vs inference. Sample limits. What could not be verified.

## 1. The brand
Identity, portfolio, corporate idea verbatim. Audited performance.
Segment and brand-level growth. Distribution. Marketing spend if disclosed.

## 2. The category, sized
Regulator and association data. Value, volume, penetration, growth.
The client's share, with the denominator stated.

## 3. The competitors — commercially
Share and trend, distribution, pricing, launches, stated strategy.

## 4. The competitors — in communication
What each is saying now. Coded consistently. Convergence, vacancy, abandonment.
Share of voice against share of market.

## 5. The audience
Who the category addresses, and who it has stopped addressing. Inference labelled.

## 6. The mechanism
Why the category behaves as it does, sourced. What it changes.

## 7. Verified, contradicted, open
The brand's published claims, each marked. Then the open questions.

## Source register
```

## Before you deliver

- Annual report and investor material read, not skimmed
- Category sized from regulator or association data before any creative judgement
- Client's claimed share checked, denominator stated, contradictions flagged
- Competitor communications collected and coded consistently, not impressionistically
- No share of voice presented from a capped or unequal sample
- Organic and paid kept separate
- Inference labelled as inference
- Every figure sourced and dated; retrieved and reported separated
- Open questions listed
