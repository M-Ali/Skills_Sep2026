# Finding the sources — consumer categories

Where the numbers live, by sector. The official statistics are usually published
by a regulator or an association and almost never rank first in a general search.

## Company filings and investor material

```
"<brand>" annual report <year> filetype:pdf
"<brand>" investor relations presentation
"<parent company>" results presentation <quarter>
site:<exchange> <ticker> announcements
```

For a listed brand, get **both** the annual report and the investor deck. The
deck is where market share, category growth and volume-versus-value splits
appear, and it is written for people who will check.

Read the analyst call transcript if there is one. Management answers questions
there that no marketing document addresses — competitive pressure, pricing,
channel conflict, which brands are underperforming.

For a multinational's local subsidiary, the **group** annual report often
discloses country-level performance that the local company does not publish.

## By sector

**Banking**
```
state bank <country> banking statistics
<country> central bank annual report banking sector
banking sector quarterly review <country>
```
Central banks publish deposit and advance shares, branch counts, and sector
profitability. This is the authoritative share data.

**Telecoms**
```
telecom authority <country> annual report
<country> telecom indicators subscribers ARPU
```
Regulators publish subscriber numbers, ARPU, teledensity and broadband
penetration, usually monthly or quarterly, by operator.

**Insurance and life assurance**
```
insurance association <country> year book
insurance regulator <country> statistics
<country> insurance industry statistics
```
Associations often publish company-by-company premium and claims data, plus
official market sizing and penetration against GDP.

**FMCG and retail**
```
<country> bureau of statistics household consumption
<category> market size <country> report
<industry> association <country>
```
Harder — often no official per-brand data. Company filings, association data and
panel measurement have to be triangulated. Say so rather than implying precision
you do not have.

## The industry association

```
<category> association <country>
<category> federation <country>
"<category>" industry statistics <country>
```

Look for:

- **A year book or annual review** — often the official market sizing, segment by
  segment, with penetration against population or GDP
- **Member-by-member statistics** — the closest thing to share data in many
  categories
- **Its own stated objectives** — where they overlap with the client's, that is a
  basis for a joint initiative rather than a parallel one

**Penetration is the number that reframes a category.** A market at low
penetration is underdeveloped rather than contested, and growth need not come
from a competitor at all. That single figure changes the strategic options, and
it is usually the association that publishes it.

## Retail and panel measurement

Nielsen, Kantar and similar provide value and volume share, penetration,
frequency and distribution. Usually subscription-only.

If the client has it, ask for it. If not, be explicit that share figures are
derived from filings and association data rather than measured, and give the
basis. An honest approximation with its method stated is worth more than a
precise-looking number nobody can trace.

## Competitor communications

Do not do this by impression. Use the sibling skills:

- **`category-creative-scan`** — resolve handles, collect live output, download
  the artwork, code every asset on a consistent schema, build the brand-by-brand
  deck and the gap analysis.
- **`competitor-comms-audit`** — message-territory mapping, share of voice
  against share of market, and how to write the finding so it survives challenge.

Two rules that matter regardless of tool:

- **A brand with no account is an absence, not a gap.** Record it and say why —
  it may be a deliberate and correct choice.
- **Check presence before choosing a platform to scan.** Collecting the wrong
  platform produces a confident reading of a channel the category does not use.
  Look at where each competitor's audience actually is before deciding what to
  collect.

## Verifying a claimed share

Brands publish shares that are old, differently based, or simply wrong. Work it
out yourself:

1. Get the **category total** from the regulator or association, with its basis
   and year.
2. Get the **client's own figure** from filings, on the same basis and year.
3. Divide, and **state the denominator**.

Watch for these traps:

- **Different bases give different answers.** The same numerator against
  "non-life conventional" versus "total market including takaful and window
  takaful" can differ by a factor of three. Both may be defensible; say which one
  you used.
- **Value versus volume share** are different claims. A brand can lead one and
  trail the other.
- **Gross versus net.** Premium written, premium earned and net premium are not
  interchangeable.
- **A ratio is not a share.** Two figures in the same currency are not
  automatically comparable if they measure different things.

## What to record

For every source:

- the **URL**, and a local copy of anything that might move
- the **retrieval date**
- **retrieved** (you read it) or **reported** (a summary said so)
- what it is **authoritative for** — a regulator on sector statistics, a panel on
  share, a filing on the company's own numbers, none of them on each other

Search snippets are routinely stale. Follower counts, store counts and shares
taken from summaries are frequently out of date, and an account a snippet still
describes may have been deleted. Read the live page before you publish a number
from it.
